About the performance

In a society that leaves its children to grow up alone, exploitation is only one step away. This story is more painfully current than we would like it to be: every human being and every creature deprived of warmth, both physical and emotional, is a “Little Match Girl” who will surely perish in the end.

We chose to tell this story through movement — the primal, visceral gesture that can sometimes matter more than the spoken word, even though Andersen’s genius cannot be ignored. While in our youth everyone knew “The Little Match Girl”, the story of the girl forced by her father to sell matches to survive, children today are more likely to know the latest computer game on the market than this literary masterpiece.

The girl is cold, barefoot and poor — without a friend in the world — but not without hope. She dreams of warmth and light, of a time when she will be surrounded by love and filled with happiness. These dreams lie so far beyond her lived experience that others would long since have given them up. But she does not give up hope.

Still, the harsh realities of poverty haunt her: she must sell matches for fear of being beaten by her father when she returns home, and this fear keeps her outside all night, which ultimately leads to her death.

We may not think about the horrible things that happen every day, and they are hard to explain to our children. It seems, however, that we can often learn the greatest lessons from children — from the way they manage to face the most uncertain situations.

In those final moments, this little girl sees visions of splendour. She sees hope. But her death — marked by a star falling across the dark sky — is tragic and disturbing.

The girl is a symbol of the countless nameless people who suffer and die in poverty every year and remain unnoticed. This is not merely a sad story but a reality check for us all. We must be grateful for everything we have and generous to those in need.

It may seem old-fashioned to try to show, through a fairy tale, all the crises of today’s society and to call them out through a secular ritual of theatre dance on a modern stage, for an audience held permanently captive by technology and the virtual world. But for us, the feelings and gestures of the characters matter more than all existing holograms and software. That is why we take up the wager with the divine, seeking to prove that human relationships, compassion and the highest ideals are not illusions controlled by satellite or drone, but tangible, real things — all the more necessary for a healthy, civilised society.
